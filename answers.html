Tree Health & Diagnosis

Why a tree looks the way it does, and what it actually means.

Why Are My Maple Tree Leaves Turning Yellow in Utah?

One of the most common causes of yellow leaves on Utah maples is iron chlorosis.

The problem is often not that Utah soil contains no iron. Utah’s alkaline soils can contain iron while making that iron difficult for susceptible plants to absorb.

A classic symptom is yellow leaf tissue with greener veins.

As chlorosis becomes more severe, leaves may become pale, develop brown or scorched edges, and eventually decline.

Maples are particularly susceptible because many commonly planted maple species are better adapted to soils with a lower pH than is common along the Wasatch Front.

But yellow leaves should not automatically be diagnosed as iron chlorosis.

Similar symptoms may also be influenced by:

  • excessive irrigation
  • poor drainage
  • compacted soil
  • root damage
  • construction
  • nutrient availability
  • plant disease
  • poor species selection
  • This is why Behunin Tree / Behunin Horticulture prefers diagnosis before treatment.

Adding fertilizer or iron without understanding the growing environment can waste money without correcting the underlying problem.

For an established valuable tree, treatment may be appropriate.

For a young tree that repeatedly develops severe chlorosis because it is poorly adapted to the site, replacing it with a better-adapted species may sometimes be the more successful long-term decision.

How Do I Know When a Tree Really Needs to Be Removed?

Tree removal should usually be based on the condition, location, and reasonably manageable risk of the tree—not simply its size or age.

Reasons removal may be appropriate include severe structural defects, extensive decay, major root instability, irreversible decline, dead canopy, repeated major failure, severe storm damage, construction conflicts that cannot reasonably be resolved, or a location that creates unacceptable long-term problems.

But not every imperfect tree needs to be removed.

Some trees can be managed through pruning, support, plant health care, irrigation correction, soil improvement, and monitoring.

Mature trees may have substantial landscape value, so preservation should be considered where practical.

The correct question is not simply, “Is something wrong with this tree?”

It is, “Can the problem be managed successfully, or has removal become the more reasonable long-term decision?”

Behunin Tree / Behunin Horticulture evaluates both preservation and removal rather than assuming one answer fits every tree.

Why Should a Tree Be Diagnosed Before It Is Fertilized or Sprayed?

Because the visible symptom is not always the actual problem.

A yellow tree might have iron chlorosis, saturated roots, poor drainage, compacted soil, construction damage, nutrient imbalance, or disease.

A thin canopy might result from drought, root loss, overwatering, insects, disease, girdling roots, or poor planting.

Applying fertilizer, insecticide, or fungicide without knowing the cause may do nothing to solve the problem.

In some cases it may make conditions worse.

Professional plant health care should begin with questions:

What is the plant doing?

What changed?

What are the soil and roots doing?

How is it being irrigated?

Are pests actually present?

Why Do Trees Decline After Construction Even When They Were Not Cut Down?

Because much of a tree is underground.

Construction can damage a tree without ever touching its trunk.

Possible impacts include soil compaction, root cutting, trenching, grade changes, paving, equipment traffic, altered drainage, irrigation changes, and soil burial.

A tree may continue looking normal for a period after construction because it has stored energy and existing foliage.

Decline can appear later.

Symptoms may include thinning canopy, smaller leaves, branch dieback, premature fall color, poor growth, and increased pest problems.

Protecting mature trees during construction should therefore include root-zone protection, not simply a fence around the trunk.

Where valuable mature trees are part of a project, they should be considered before grading, utility placement, hardscape, or excavation begins.

Irrigation & Water Management

Watering trees, lawns, and landscapes in Utah's climate.

Can an Overwatered Tree Look Like It Needs More Water?

Yes.

This is one of the easiest landscape problems to misdiagnose.

A homeowner sees wilting, leaf scorch, yellow foliage, thinning canopy, or poor growth and assumes the tree needs more water.

But saturated soil can create many of the same visible symptoms.

Roots need both water and oxygen.

When soil remains saturated for extended periods, the air spaces within the soil fill with water.

Roots can lose access to enough oxygen, decline, and become less capable of absorbing water.

The strange result is that a tree surrounded by wet soil can develop symptoms that resemble drought stress.

Adding even more irrigation can make the problem worse.

This is especially important in heavy clay soils, poorly drained landscapes, low areas, lawns watered frequently, newly planted trees, and properties with leaking irrigation.

Before changing irrigation, check the root zone.

The real question is not, “How long did the sprinklers run?”

It is, “What is the moisture condition where the roots are actually growing?”

JLB Canopy Management treats irrigation and plant physiology as connected issues rather than separate services.

Should I Remove Mature Trees to Save Water in Utah?

Not automatically.

Mature trees use water, but they also provide benefits that may take decades to replace.

Depending on the property, mature trees can provide shade, cooler outdoor areas, reduced solar exposure on buildings, privacy, habitat, landscape character, and substantial replacement value.

A mature landscape should therefore be evaluated before assuming removal is the best water-conservation strategy.

The better first question may be:

Can we reduce unnecessary plant water demand while preserving the valuable canopy?

That could involve:

  • correcting excessive irrigation
  • pruning appropriately
  • managing canopy density
  • improving soil conditions
  • separating irrigation zones
  • managing unnecessary vegetative growth
  • changing turf or planting areas where appropriate
  • Occasionally, removal will still be appropriate.

A tree may be unsafe, poorly adapted, chronically unhealthy, incorrectly located, or incompatible with the future landscape.

But water conservation alone does not automatically make a healthy mature tree a liability.

JLB Canopy Management starts with plant demand and evaluates the entire landscape before deciding what should stay, change, or be removed.

Does Pruning a Tree Reduce Its Water Use?

Pruning changes a tree’s canopy and leaf area, so it can affect transpiration and plant water demand.

But that does not mean that removing an arbitrary percentage of branches produces the same percentage reduction in landscape water use.

Trees are biological systems.

Their response depends on species, amount of foliage removed, pruning method, season, regrowth, soil, irrigation, weather, and plant health.

Heavy pruning may also trigger vigorous regrowth, damage structure, or create unnecessary stress.

That is why JLB does not use a strategy of simply “cutting more tree to save more water.”

The preferred approach is precision canopy management.

That means selecting appropriate branches, making cuts at proper branch origins, preserving natural form, and managing canopy density without unnecessarily stripping the tree.

Then irrigation can be evaluated to determine whether the landscape’s actual demand has changed.

Behunin Tree / Behunin Horticulture considers pruning, irrigation, soil, physiology, microclimate, growth, and maintenance together.

The objective is not just a smaller canopy.

It is a better-managed landscape.

Should Trees Be Watered the Same Way as Lawn?

Usually not.

Turfgrass and established trees have very different root systems and irrigation needs.

Turf generally has a shallower root zone and often receives water more frequently.

Trees and large shrubs typically benefit from moisture reaching deeper into the soil profile.

Problems occur when an irrigation system treats them as if they were the same plant.

A tree growing inside lawn may receive frequent shallow irrigation because the lawn needs it.

That does not automatically mean the tree’s deeper root system is being watered correctly.

It can also create chronically wet surface soil around trees that prefer more drying between irrigation cycles.

Good irrigation management considers root depth, soil texture, species, establishment stage, weather, sun, shade, and sprinkler distribution.

Where practical, turf and woody plants should be managed according to their different water requirements.

That is one reason irrigation zoning is an important part of JLB Canopy Management.

Why Should Irrigation Change After Tree Pruning or Removal?

Because the landscape has changed.

A mature tree affects far more than the space occupied by its trunk.

Its canopy influences shade, temperature, wind, evaporation, soil moisture, surrounding shrubs, turf, and sprinkler patterns.

When a large canopy is substantially changed or a tree is removed, the plants below and around it may suddenly receive much more sun.

Soil may dry faster.

Turf may respond differently.

Plants that previously grew in shade may experience heat stress.

At the same time, part of the plant water demand has disappeared.

Leaving irrigation exactly as it was before the tree work may no longer make sense.

That is why JLB connects canopy management and irrigation.

Tree work should not always end when the branches leave the property.

The resulting landscape should be evaluated to determine whether its irrigation, plant selection, or maintenance should change as well.

Does a Water-Wise Utah Landscape Have to Eliminate the Lawn?

No.

Turfgrass can be a practical part of a water-conscious landscape when it has a purpose and is irrigated appropriately.

Useful turf may provide children’s play space, pet areas, recreation, gathering space, cooling, open visual space, and relatively simple maintenance.

The better question is not, “Should every lawn disappear?”

It is, “Is turf being used where turf makes sense?”

Narrow strips, difficult slopes, awkward corners, or lawn that serves no useful purpose may be candidates for redesign.

But replacing a large functional lawn with a very large planting bed can also increase weeds, pruning, plant replacement, mulch maintenance, and irrigation complexity.

Water-wise design should evaluate the whole property.

Turf can remain where it serves a useful role while irrigation, grass selection, soil, and maintenance are improved.

JLB focuses on appropriate plant demand, not simply reducing the amount of living vegetation.

Does Hybrid or Improved Bluegrass Use Less Water?

Some improved and hybrid bluegrass cultivars have better drought or heat performance than older conventional selections.

But the important word is: some.

“Hybrid bluegrass” is not a guarantee that every seed blend will use a specific percentage less water.

Performance depends on cultivar, climate, soil, mowing, irrigation, establishment, and desired appearance.

That is why Behunin Tree / Behunin Horticulture prefers to tie water-use claims to the actual seed blend being installed rather than making one blanket percentage claim for every improved grass.

Seed selection can be an important water-management tool.

But it should be combined with appropriate turf placement, soil preparation, correct mowing, irrigation zoning, and seasonal scheduling.

The grass and the irrigation system need to work together.

Do Plant Growth Regulators Save Water?

Growth regulation can be part of a broader strategy to reduce unnecessary plant demand, but it would be misleading to promise a fixed landscape water-savings percentage from a PGR treatment alone.

Plant growth and water use are related.

Leaves are major locations of transpiration, and managing excessive vegetative growth can change the way a plant allocates resources.

But actual landscape water demand also depends on species, canopy, weather, irrigation, soil, microclimate, plant health, and surrounding vegetation.

That is why JLB does not treat PGRs as a stand-alone water-conservation product.

Growth management is combined with canopy management, irrigation optimization, soil management, plant physiology, microclimate, and long-term maintenance.

The goal is to change the landscape intelligently and then adjust irrigation to match actual plant demand.

Should Turf, Trees, and Shrubs Be on Separate Irrigation Zones?

Where practical, grouping plants by similar water requirements makes irrigation much easier to manage.

Turf, mature trees, newly planted trees, and shrub beds may all require different watering frequency, depth, application method, and seasonal adjustment.

When very different plants share one irrigation zone, the property may end up watering one group excessively just to keep another alive.

For example, a lawn may benefit from a different watering pattern than an established tree’s root zone.

A new tree may require special establishment irrigation.

A shaded shrub bed may need less water than a bed exposed to afternoon sun.

This is known as hydrozoning—grouping landscape areas according to their irrigation needs.

Pruning, Canopy & JLB

How and why trees are pruned, and what canopy management does.

Why Does Shearing Shrubs Make Them Grow Back So Thick?

Many shrubs respond to repeated shearing by producing dense new growth near the outer surface of the plant.

The cycle often looks like this:

grow → shear → regrow → shear again.

Over time, the outside of the shrub may become very dense while the interior becomes shaded and less productive.

This can create constant maintenance, unnatural shape, poor interior structure, reduced airflow, repeated green waste, and shrubs that look too large again only weeks after trimming.

The underlying problem may not actually be pruning.

Sometimes the plant is simply too large for the space where it was installed.

If a shrub naturally wants to grow six feet tall, repeatedly forcing it to remain two feet tall creates a permanent maintenance conflict.

Better long-term options may include selective pruning rather than exterior shearing, reducing unnecessary irrigation or fertilization, professional growth management, replacing an incorrectly sized plant, or designing new landscapes around mature plant size.

JLB treats shrub management as part of plant physiology, irrigation, growth management, and long-term maintenance—not merely as trimming.

In appropriate plants, professional plant growth regulators can reduce shoot elongation and help slow excessive vegetative growth.

Planting, Turf & Landscape

Choosing, planting, transplanting, and establishing.

Is Hydroseeding Better Than Sod?

Neither method is automatically better for every project.

Sod provides an almost immediate finished lawn.

Hydroseeding requires germination and establishment.

However, hydroseeding can offer major advantages depending on the project.

These may include lower installation cost than sod, flexibility in seed selection, ability to choose blends suited to the property, efficient installation over larger areas, and good soil contact when properly prepared.

The largest mistake is treating either sod or hydroseed as a substitute for good preparation.

A successful lawn still depends on grading, soil preparation, drainage, irrigation coverage, appropriate grass selection, and establishment watering.

Hydroseeding will not correct compacted soil, poor drainage, or a badly designed irrigation system.

Why Does Tree Planting Depth Matter?

Because a tree can be planted incorrectly on the first day and struggle with that mistake for decades.

The base of the trunk naturally widens where major roots begin.

This area is commonly called the root flare or root collar.

It should generally remain near the finished soil surface rather than being deeply buried.

When a tree is planted too deep, potential problems can include reduced oxygen around roots, trunk tissue buried in wet soil, girdling-root development, poor root establishment, and long-term decline.

A common mistake is assuming the top of the nursery container or root ball automatically represents the correct planting depth.

Sometimes the root flare has already been buried during nursery production.

It should be located before final planting depth is established.

The planting hole should generally be wide rather than excessively deep, because most tree roots spread laterally through relatively shallow soil.

Good tree establishment begins with the root system.

Should Every Newly Planted Tree Be Staked?

No.

Some newly planted trees need temporary support.

Others are stable enough without it.

When staking is necessary, the purpose is to help stabilize the root system while still allowing the tree some normal movement.

A tree should not be rigidly tied forever.

Problems can occur when stakes or ties are forgotten.

As the tree grows, ties may damage bark, restrict the trunk, become embedded, or interfere with normal development.

The decision should consider tree size, root ball stability, wind exposure, site conditions, and planting method.

Staking is a temporary establishment tool—not a permanent part of the tree.

Can a Large Mature Tree Be Transplanted?

Sometimes.

Large-tree transplanting can be possible, but the decision depends on much more than tree height.

Important factors include species, tree condition, trunk size, root system, root-ball size, soil, equipment access, distance of the move, destination site, timing, irrigation, and expected survival.

Larger trees require substantial root systems to support their canopies.

Moving them generally means removing part of the existing root system, so careful planning and aftercare become critical.

Behunin Tree / Behunin Horticulture has experience moving exceptionally large trees, including trees over 60 feet tall with root balls spanning approximately 22 feet.

But transplanting is not automatically the right decision just because it is technically possible.

The value of preserving the tree, likelihood of establishment, project cost, destination, and long-term landscape plan should all be evaluated.

Sometimes preservation in place is best.

Sometimes transplanting makes sense.

Sometimes removal and replacement is the more practical option.

When Is the Best Time to Plant a Tree in Utah?

For many trees, early spring or fall can be excellent planting periods.

Cooler temperatures can reduce stress while roots begin establishing.

Spring planting is particularly useful because the plant has much of the growing season ahead of it.

Fall can also work well for many species if soil moisture is maintained and the plant has time to begin establishment before severe winter conditions.

Summer planting is possible, particularly with container or balled-and-burlapped material, but hot temperatures increase the importance of careful irrigation.

Timing is only one part of successful planting.

Other critical factors include correct species, proper root flare depth, adequate root ball, soil, irrigation, drainage, and aftercare.

A correctly planted tree at an appropriate time still needs proper establishment management afterward.

Utah Microclimates & Elevation

Why the right answer changes between the valley and the mountains.

Why Are Tree and Landscape Choices Different in Park City, Kamas, Oakley, and Heber?

Elevation changes the growing environment.

Summit and Wasatch County landscapes can experience colder winter temperatures, shorter growing seasons, later spring frosts, earlier fall frosts, heavier snow, strong wind, different soil, and greater exposure.

A plant that performs well in Salt Lake Valley may be poorly adapted to a mountain property.

High-elevation plant selection should consider cold hardiness, mature size, snow loading, wind, slope, sun exposure, irrigation, and local microclimate.

Even two areas of the same property can behave differently.

A protected courtyard may stay warmer and retain moisture differently from an exposed hillside.

That is why JLB’s microclimate component becomes especially important in high-elevation landscapes.

Do Rock, Concrete, and Hardscape Make Landscapes Hotter?

Yes.

Landscape surfaces influence microclimate.

Areas surrounded by pavement, concrete, masonry, walls, rock, or buildings may absorb and reradiate heat differently from areas with living vegetation and shade.

That can influence leaf temperature, soil temperature, evaporation, irrigation demand, plant stress, and human comfort.

This does not mean hardscape is bad.

Patios, walks, retaining features, outdoor kitchens, pools, and decorative stone all have important landscape functions.

The issue is integration.

A plant beside a west-facing wall and pavement may experience a very different environment from the same species growing beneath a mature tree.

JLB treats microclimate as one of seven core landscape factors.

That means plants, irrigation, shade, hardscape, and mature canopy should be considered together.
